The esophagus is a muscular tube that carries swallowed food to the stomach. The esophagus begins at the pharynx, or throat, and stays collapsed when you are not swallowing. Along the way down, the esophagus lies behind the trachea, or windpipe. The esophagus then passes through an opening in the diaphragm, a dome-shaped breathing muscle, and empties into the stomach. Peristalsis, waves of muscle contraction and relaxation, starts in the upper esophagus and pushes food toward the stomach. During peristalsis, a circular muscle layer squeezes the wall to force the food forward, while a lengthwise layer shortens the wall and widens it to receive the food. Upper and lower sphincters, muscles that ring the tube and act as valves, control food moving into and out of the esophagus. The lower esophageal sphincter relaxes to let food into the stomach, then contracts to keep stomach acid from backing up into the esophagus. A tissue called stratified squamous epithelium, made of many layers of cells, lines the esophagus and protects it from wear by food. Glands in the inner lining release mucus, a thick, slippery substance that keeps the lining moist and helps food pass.
