An eosinophil is a white blood cell that helps protect against helminths, or parasitic worms, and works with other eosinophils to surround a parasite. Found in the blood, eosinophils help promote inflammation, a basic immune response. However, eosinophils also release antihistamines, molecules that work against histamine, an inflammatory chemical made by other immune cells. Eosinophils typically make up 2–4 percent of all white blood cells and measure 10–12 µm in diameter. An eosinophil is a granular leukocyte: a white blood cell with many granules, which are stores of chemicals inside the cell. These granules stain red to orange with an acidic dye called eosin, and the cell's nucleus typically has two to three lobes. Some eosinophil granules hold molecules that are toxic to parasitic worms. When worms settle in the digestive tract, chemical messengers called cytokines draw eosinophils to the site, where the eosinophils release their granule contents. Eosinophils can also engulf large pathogens, disease-causing agents such as bacteria and fungi. Eosinophils arise from myeloid stem cells, which also give rise to red blood cells and some other white blood cells.
