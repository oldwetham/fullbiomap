Hepatocytes, the main cell type of the liver, process, store and release nutrients into the blood. Hepatocytes make up around 80 percent of the liver's volume. They are arranged in hepatic lobules, six-sided units in which the cells radiate outward from a central vein. Hepatocytes are tightly packed around hepatic sinusoids, the liver's open blood spaces, so the cells have easy access to the blood. Hepatocytes either pass glucose on through the bloodstream or store the excess as glycogen, the stored form of glucose. In addition, hepatocytes make many toxins harmless, including toxins taken in from the environment and harmful by-products of the body's own cells. Hepatocytes secrete about one liter a day of bile, a digestive juice that is important for digesting fats. The bile collects in bile canaliculi, small ducts that lie in grooves in the membranes of neighboring hepatocytes. Processed materials such as bilirubin, a waste product from old red blood cells, are excreted into the bile canaliculi.
