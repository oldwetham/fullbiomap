Rod cells are specialized for vision in low light and are one of the two types of photoreceptor, cells that respond to light. Rods lie in the outer edges of the retina, the layer of light-sensing and supporting cells lining the back of the eye. Rods are more sensitive to light than cones, the photoreceptors for color vision, but rod vision is less sharp. In a rod, the inner segment holds the nucleus and other organelles, the cell's small working parts, while the outer segment is where light is detected. The outer segment, shaped like a rod, holds a stack of membrane discs that contain rhodopsin, the light-sensitive pigment of rods. Rhodopsin has two main parts: a membrane protein called an opsin, and retinal, a molecule that absorbs light and changes shape when light hits the rod. This shape change sets off a chain of signals that makes the rod release less neurotransmitter, a chemical signal. The rod releases this neurotransmitter onto bipolar cells, the cells that pass visual signals on from rods and cones. In darkness, rods constantly hold back these bipolar cells. Light lifts this hold, so the bipolar cells stimulate ganglion cells, which send signals out of the eye through the optic nerve.
