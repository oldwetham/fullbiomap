CD4-positive T cells are white blood cells linked mainly to the work of helper T cells. Helper T cells strengthen other immune responses by releasing cytokines, molecules that carry messages between nearby cells. Regulatory T cells, however, are a class of CD4 T cells that keep other T cell responses in check. When T cells leave the thymus, the organ where they multiply and mature, each carries either CD4 or CD8 but not both. T cells travel in the blood and in lymph, a watery fluid that bathes tissues, and live in organs such as the spleen and lymph nodes. T cells recognize antigens, foreign proteins that trigger an immune response, with a receptor made of two protein chains; the most common kind is the alpha-beta T cell receptor. A CD4-positive, alpha-beta T cell is a mature T cell with an alpha-beta T cell receptor and the CD4 coreceptor, a molecule that helps the cell bind the right target. CD4 also keeps the T cell in close contact with an antigen-presenting cell, an immune cell that shows processed antigens on its surface. A CD4-positive T cell binds antigen-presenting cells through their MHC class II molecules, proteins that present antigens, and can then become a helper T cell.
