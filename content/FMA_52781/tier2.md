The external ear collects sound waves and funnels them through the ear canal to the eardrum. The external ear lies on the side of the head and includes the ear canal and the auricle, the fleshy outer part of the ear. Some sources call the auricle the pinna, but that name better fits an ear that can move, such as a cat's. The external ear contains elastic cartilage, a type of cartilage that gives firm support yet can stretch and spring back. The auricle's C-shaped curves steer incoming sound waves toward the ear canal. Because sound from one spot reaches the two auricles at slightly different times, the brain can use this gap to work out where the sound came from. The ear canal passes into the skull through an opening in the temporal bone, one of the paired bones that form the lower sides of the skull. At the inner end of the ear canal lies the eardrum, or tympanic membrane, a thin sheet that vibrates when sound waves strike it. The vibrating eardrum passes the sound's energy on to the middle ear, a space in the temporal bone where the sound waves are amplified.
