Multiciliated cells use their cilia, short hair-like parts, to help clear the airways. These cells line the trachea, or windpipe, which is the main airway to the lungs, and the bronchi that branch from the trachea. Goblet cells in the same lining make mucus, a sticky fluid that traps particles. Each multiciliated cell is column-shaped and carries many cilia on its apical surface, the side that faces the open airway. Each cilium is a tiny extension of the cell's apical membrane, supported by microtubules, structural filaments made of a protein called tubulin. In each cilium, nine pairs of microtubules form a ring around a central pair, an arrangement called the 9 + 2 array. The cilia beat in unison and move mucus and particles back up to the throat, where the mucus is swallowed. This constant upward push of mucus gives the ciliated lining its name, the mucociliary escalator.
