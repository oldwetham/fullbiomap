A keratinocyte makes and stores keratin, a fibrous protein that gives skin, hair and nails their hardness and water resistance. By making keratin and releasing germ-fighting substances, keratinocytes form a barrier against damage, water loss and germs. Melanin, the pigment that colors skin and hair, builds up in keratinocytes; this darkens the skin and protects the DNA of skin cells from UV rays. Keratinocytes are found in stratified squamous tissues, such as the skin and the lining of the mouth; these tissues have many cell layers, with flat, scale-like cells on top. In the skin, keratinocytes are the most common cells of the epidermis, the skin's outermost layer. All keratinocytes in the epidermis come from basal cells, stem cells that keep dividing in a single layer. In the stratum spinosum, the layer above the basal cells, keratinocytes start making keratin and release a water-repelling substance that makes the skin fairly waterproof. Pushed farther up as the basal layer keeps making new cells, keratinocytes become flatter, grow thicker cell membranes and make large amounts of keratin. As these keratinocytes die, their nuclei and other organelles, the small working parts of a cell, break down, leaving keratin behind. In the stratum corneum, the top layer of the epidermis, dead keratinocytes regularly flake away and are replaced by cells from deeper in the epidermis.
