Principal cells help control how much sodium, potassium and water the body keeps or loses. These cells line the collecting ducts, the kidney tubes that carry urine away for excretion. The duct lining is simple cuboidal or columnar epithelium, a single layer of cells that are either boxy or taller than they are wide. Principal cells respond to two hormones: ADH, which signals the kidneys to take back water, and aldosterone, which controls how much sodium is recovered. When stimulated by ADH, principal cells insert aquaporins, proteins that form water channels, into the membrane facing the inside of the duct. Water is then pulled out of the duct into the surrounding tissue and nearby blood capillaries, so the final urine is more concentrated. But when less ADH is released, fewer channels are inserted, less water is recovered, and the urine is dilute. Aldosterone makes principal cells build channels and pumps that move sodium and potassium. When aldosterone rises, more sodium is recovered from the forming urine and water follows the sodium, while potassium is pumped into the urine.
