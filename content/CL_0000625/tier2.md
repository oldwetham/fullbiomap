CD8-positive T cells are white blood cells that can become cytotoxic T cells, which kill infected cells directly. When T cells leave the thymus, the organ where they multiply and mature, each carries either CD4 or CD8 but not both. T cells travel in the blood and in lymph, a watery fluid that bathes tissues, and live in organs such as the spleen and lymph nodes. T cells recognize antigens, foreign proteins that trigger an immune response, with a receptor made of two protein chains; the most common kind is the alpha-beta T cell receptor. A CD8-positive, alpha-beta T cell is a T cell with an alpha-beta T cell receptor and the CD8 coreceptor, a molecule that helps the cell bind the right target. CD8 binds to MHC class I, a protein that presents antigens to T cells and is found on most cells of the body. Cytotoxic T cells kill target cells by triggering apoptosis, a controlled form of cell death. When a cytotoxic T cell's receptor binds an antigen on an infected cell, the T cell releases perforin and granzyme, which make the infected cell undergo apoptosis. Perforin makes a pore in the target cell, and granzyme enters the cell through that pore. Each cytotoxic T cell can kill more than one target cell.
