The appendix may play a part in immunity, because it contains lymphoid tissue, clusters of immune cells called lymphocytes. In the inner layers of the appendix wall, this tissue takes part in sampling material from inside the gut, so the body can mount immune responses to potential pathogens, agents that can cause disease. Even so, the appendix is generally considered vestigial, a structure with no apparent function that seems left over from a working part in a distant ancestor. The appendix is a winding tube about 7.6 cm (3 in) long, attached to the cecum, the pouch that forms the beginning of the large intestine. Also called the vermiform, or worm-like, appendix, it sits at the ileocecal valve, where the ileum, the end of the small intestine, gives way to the large intestine. A mesentery is a double sheet of membrane that suspends digestive organs; the appendix's own mesentery, the mesoappendix, tethers the appendix to the mesentery of the ileum. The appendix's twisted shape gives gut bacteria a sheltered place to gather and multiply.
