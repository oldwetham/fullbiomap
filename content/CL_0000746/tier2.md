Cardiac muscle cells, or cardiomyocytes, make up the heart muscle and carry out the heart's pumping. Together they form the myocardium, the middle layer and the bulk of the heart wall. Under a microscope they look striated, or striped, and each typically has a single nucleus in its center. Their plentiful mitochondria, the cell parts that produce energy, power the heart's contractions. The cells branch and join end to end at intercalated discs, the junctions between neighboring cardiac muscle cells. Gap junctions in the discs form channels between neighboring cells, so electrical impulses spread from cell to cell and the cells contract in a coordinated way. The rest of each disc is made of desmosomes, structures that anchor the ends of the cells together so they do not pull apart as they contract. Cardiomyocytes can also contract in their own rhythm without outside stimulation, an ability called autorhythmicity. Most cardiac muscle cells are myocardial contractile cells, which contract to propel blood. Myocardial conducting cells, the other major type, carry electrical impulses through the heart and trigger those contractions.
