Taste receptor cells sense chemicals in the food we eat and release neurotransmitters, chemical signals, based on how much of each chemical is present. These cells, also called taste cells, cluster in taste buds, which sit within bumps on the tongue called papillae. Taste cells are long cells whose hair-like tips, called microvilli, reach into a pore in the taste bud. A taste bud's taste cells are replaced every 10 to 14 days. Food molecules dissolve in saliva and then bind to receptors, proteins with a binding site for a specific molecule, on the microvilli. Each of the five primary tastes (sweet, sour, bitter, salty and umami, a savory taste) has its own type of receptor that responds only to that taste. Sour and salty tastes come from ions, atoms with an electric charge, entering the taste cell: hydrogen ions for sour and sodium ions for salty. Sweet, bitter and umami tastes instead need a G protein–coupled receptor, a receptor that activates a G protein to pass the signal on inside the cell. In a taste cell, a change in voltage across the cell membrane leads to the release of neurotransmitters where the cell meets sensory nerve cells. These neurotransmitters can activate sensory nerve cells in the facial, glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, which are cranial nerves, nerves connected to the brain.
