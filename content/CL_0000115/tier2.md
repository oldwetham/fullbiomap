Endothelial cells form the smooth inner lining of blood vessels and are in contact with the red blood cells. This lining is called the endothelium, and it continues throughout the blood vessels and the chambers of the heart. The endothelium is a simple squamous epithelium, which means a single layer of flat, scale-like cells. In blood capillaries, the smallest blood vessels, small molecules such as gases pass directly through the endothelial cells. Some capillaries also have pores in their endothelial lining, and these pores let larger molecules through. Vesicles, small membrane-bound sacs, can also carry some large blood proteins into and out of endothelial cells. Endothelial cells also form lymph capillaries, the smallest lymphatic vessels, which carry fluid away from the tissues. In lymph capillaries, the endothelial cells overlap to form a one-way valve that lets fluid in but not back out. Endothelial cells release nitric oxide, which causes vasodilation, the widening of blood vessels. However, endothelial cells also release endothelins, local chemicals that cause vasoconstriction, the narrowing of blood vessels.
