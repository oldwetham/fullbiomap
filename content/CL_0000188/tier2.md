Skeletal muscle cells, long cylinders commonly called muscle fibers, contract to pull on bones and move the skeleton. These cells make up skeletal muscle, which is usually attached to bone and under voluntary, or conscious, control. Each fiber has many nuclei because it forms when many myoblasts, cells that form muscle, fuse together. Inside each fiber, the proteins actin and myosin are arranged in a very regular pattern of stripes, called striations. One fiber holds hundreds to thousands of myofibrils, long cylindrical structures that run parallel to the fiber. Each myofibril is a chain of many sarcomeres, the repeating units that carry out contraction. Every fiber is controlled by a motor neuron, a nerve cell that carries signals from the brain or spinal cord to the muscle. A signal from the motor neuron makes the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the fiber's calcium store, release calcium. The calcium moves shielding proteins aside, so myosin heads can attach to actin and pull it inward, shortening the fiber. Each pull by the myosin heads uses energy from ATP, the cell's energy currency.
