The main job of each lung is gas exchange: swapping oxygen and carbon dioxide with the outside air. The lungs lie in the chest cavity, which also holds the heart in the mediastinum, the space between the lungs. The right lung has three lobes, the smaller units a lung is made of, and is shorter, wider and larger than the left lung. The left lung, in contrast, is longer and narrower, with two lobes and a dent called the cardiac notch that makes room for the tip of the heart. Each lung is attached only at the hilum, a point on the lung's inner side where the bronchi, the tubes that carry air in and out, enter with blood vessels and nerves. The bronchi branch into smaller and smaller passages that end in alveoli, tiny grape-like sacs in the lung where gas exchange takes place. Oxygen passes from the alveoli into the blood, while carbon dioxide, a waste product of the body's cells, passes from the blood into the alveoli to be breathed out. Each lung is enclosed in the pleura, a two-layered membrane: one layer is firmly attached to the lung, and the other lines the chest wall. The lungs do not move by themselves: sticky pleural fluid between the two layers makes the lungs follow the chest wall outward when we breathe in. Normal breathing out takes no energy, because the elastic lung tissue springs back as the breathing muscles relax.
