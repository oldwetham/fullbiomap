The stomach helps digest food from the esophagus, both mechanically and chemically, turning the food into chyme, a soupy liquid of food mixed with digestive juices. The stomach holds the chyme and releases it gradually into the small intestine through the pyloric sphincter, a muscle around the passage that acts as a valve. The only stomach function that is necessary for life is making intrinsic factor, which the intestine needs in order to absorb vitamin B12. The stomach lies between the liver and the spleen, under the diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle beneath the lungs. The stomach has four main regions, the cardia, fundus, body and pylorus; food enters through the cardia, where the esophagus joins the stomach. Besides the usual circular and lengthwise muscle layers, the stomach wall has an inner oblique layer, running at an angle, that lets the stomach churn food vigorously and break it into smaller pieces. A vast number of gastric pits, narrow channels in the stomach lining, lead into gastric glands, which secrete a digestive fluid called gastric juice. The stomach's chief cells release pepsin, an enzyme (a protein that speeds up chemical reactions) that digests protein, in an inactive form called pepsinogen. Hydrochloric acid, a digestive acid secreted by the stomach's parietal cells, is needed to activate pepsin and also kills much of the bacteria swallowed with food. The stomach is kept from digesting itself by a mucosal barrier, which includes a thick coat of mucus whose bicarbonate neutralizes acid.
