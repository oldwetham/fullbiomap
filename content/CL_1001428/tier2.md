Bladder urothelial cells keep urine from leaking out of the urinary bladder, which they line. The lining they form is also built to handle the bladder's large changes in volume. This lining is called transitional epithelium because its surface cells gradually change shape as the bladder fills with urine. Transitional, or uroepithelial, cells like these occur only in the urinary system, mainly in the bladder and in the ureters, which carry urine from the kidneys. The cells form a stratified layer, meaning one made of several layers of cells. In an empty bladder, the lining is folded, and the cells on the lining's surface are cuboidal, or boxy, with domed, umbrella-shaped tops. But as the bladder fills, the folds smooth out, and the surface cells change from cuboidal to squamous, meaning flattened and thin. The lining looks thicker and more layered in an empty bladder, but more stretched out and less layered in a full one. Neighboring cells in this lining are joined by tight junctions, watertight seals that stop urine from leaking out into the space around the cells.
