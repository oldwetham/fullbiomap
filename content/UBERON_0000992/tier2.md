Each ovary makes oocytes, or eggs, and sex hormones, notably estrogen and progesterone. The ovaries lie in shallow hollows in the side walls of the pelvic cavity, one on each side of the uterus. Each ovary is about the size and shape of an almond. The ovary's tissue is divided into an outer cortex and an inner medulla. In the cortex, each oocyte develops surrounded by supporting cells, and an oocyte with its supporting cells is called a follicle. The medulla, beneath the cortex, holds the ovary's blood vessels, lymph vessels and nerves. At birth the ovaries hold one to two million oocytes, and the number declines throughout life until menopause, the end of reproductive function, when no follicles remain. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) promotes the growth of follicles, which then release estrogens. About every 28 days, one follicle releases its oocyte from the ovary, an event called ovulation, while several other follicles die. After ovulation, the follicle left behind becomes the corpus luteum, which makes large amounts of progesterone, a hormone critical for establishing and maintaining pregnancy.
