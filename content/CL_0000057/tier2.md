A fibroblast makes the fibers found in nearly all connective tissues, the tissues that hold in place and connect the body's organs. Fibroblasts are the most abundant cells in connective tissue. Fibroblasts secrete three main kinds of fibers: collagen fibers (flexible protein fibers that give connective tissue strength), elastic fibers and reticular fibers. Elastic fibers are rich in the protein elastin, which lets them stretch and then return to their original size. Reticular fibers are fine protein fibers made of collagen subunits that link together to form supporting nets. In addition, fibroblasts secrete polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates) and proteins that mix with the fluid outside the cells to form a thick ground substance. With the fibers, this ground substance forms the extracellular matrix, material that protects and anchors the cells of the tissue. Fibroblasts can move, divide and make whichever connective tissue is needed. In the skin, fibroblasts produce the mesh of collagen and elastic fibers in the dermis, a layer of skin that contains blood vessels, hair follicles and sweat glands. In the synovial membrane, the lining of a freely movable joint, fibroblasts release hyaluronan, a substance that traps water to form synovial fluid, a lubricant that lets the bones of the joint move freely.
