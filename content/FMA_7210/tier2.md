Each testis makes sperm and androgens, the hormones that support male reproduction. Each testis sits in its own compartment of the scrotum, an external pouch of skin and muscle. A tough, white layer called the tunica albuginea covers each testis and folds inward, dividing the testis into sections called lobules. Inside the lobules, sperm develop in seminiferous tubules, the tightly coiled tubes that make up most of each testis. Between the seminiferous tubules lie Leydig cells, which make the androgen testosterone. Within the tubules, sperm come from stem cells called spermatogonia, which divide so that one daughter cell becomes a sperm cell and the other gives rise to the next generation of sperm. As sperm cells develop, they are pushed toward the tubule's hollow center, called the lumen. Large Sertoli cells, found among the developing cells, reach from the tubule's outer edge to the lumen and support and nourish the other cells. Newly formed sperm are released into the lumen and moved along a series of ducts to the epididymis, a coiled tube attached to the testis. However, these sperm are still immature and cannot fertilize an egg; they finish maturing as they move through the epididymis.
