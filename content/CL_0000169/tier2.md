Beta cells make insulin, a hormone that lowers the amount of glucose, a simple sugar, in the blood. Beta cells sit in the pancreatic islets, or islets of Langerhans: clusters of hormone-releasing cells in the pancreas, an organ between the stomach and the small intestine. Beta cells make up about 75 percent of each islet. When food reaches the intestine, the gut releases hormones that give beta cells the first signal to make and release insulin. Once nutrients are absorbed, the surge in blood glucose stimulates beta cells to release even more insulin. Inside a beta cell, a rise in calcium ions (Ca2+) is a signal that leads to the release of insulin. Insulin lowers blood glucose: most body cells take up glucose faster, and skeletal muscles and the liver build more glycogen, the stored form of glucose. As insulin brings blood glucose down, the lower level holds back further insulin release from beta cells; this negative feedback loop keeps the response from going too far.
