Thyroid follicular cells make, store and release thyroid hormones, chemical signals that control how the body works. These hormones, thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), set the body's basal metabolic rate, the amount of energy the body needs at rest. Most of the thyroid gland is made of follicles; in each follicle, a wall of follicular cells surrounds a cavity filled with a sticky fluid called colloid. Each follicular cell is an epithelial cell, part of the tissue that lines or covers organs. A follicular cell's shape shows how active the cell is: flat when inactive, but cube-shaped or column-shaped when active. The cell's surface that faces the colloid is covered with microvilli, projections that increase a cell's surface area. To make thyroid hormones, follicular cells need iodide, a charged form of iodine that comes from the diet. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), a hormone from the front part of the pituitary gland, makes the cells pump iodide in from the blood. The cells release a protein called thyroglobulin into the colloid, where iodine atoms attach to thyroglobulin to form the hormones. TSH also makes the cells take colloid back inside, where lysosomes, the cell's digestive parts, break thyroglobulin apart and free T3 and T4 to enter the blood.
