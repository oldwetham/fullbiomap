Platelets play a key part in hemostasis, the way the body seals a torn blood vessel and stops further blood loss. A platelet is not a true cell but a membrane-wrapped fragment of a megakaryocyte, a bone marrow cell that makes platelets. After platelets enter the blood, about one-third move to the spleen, an organ that filters blood, and are stored there for release when a blood vessel tears. Each platelet is a disc 2 to 4 micrometers across that holds many small vesicles, or membrane-bound sacs, but no nucleus. At a torn vessel, platelets clump together, turn spiky and sticky, and bind to the exposed vessel wall. This mass of stuck platelets, called a platelet plug, can seal a small hole in a vessel for a short time while longer-lasting repairs are made. As platelets gather, they release chemicals from their granules, small vesicles that hold material for release, and these chemicals further help stop the bleeding. Along with the liver, platelets are the main source of clotting factors, the substances active in blood clotting. Once a clot forms, proteins inside the platelets contract and tug on the clot's fibrin threads, the protein strands that make up the clot, drawing its edges more tightly together. Platelets also release growth factors, molecules that bind to cells and stimulate cell growth, and these factors are essential for tissue growth and repair.
