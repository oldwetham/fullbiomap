Each intervertebral disk strongly joins two neighboring vertebrae, the individual bones of the neck and back. The disk pads the vertebrae during weight bearing, and its ability to change shape lets them move. The disk fills the gap between two vertebral bodies, the front part of each vertebra that bears body weight. Each disk is a thick pad of fibrocartilage, a tough form of cartilage. Because fibrocartilage contains bundles of thick collagen fibers, which are flexible proteins, it is very strong and resists pulling and bending forces. The disk's tough, fibrous outer ring, the anulus fibrosus, is firmly anchored to the outer edges of the neighboring vertebral bodies. Inside the ring is the nucleus pulposus, a softer gel whose high water content resists squeezing and so helps bear weight. This gel lets the disk change shape when one vertebra rocks forward, back or side to side against a neighboring vertebra. For example, bending forward squeezes the front of the disk but expands the back of the disk.
