B cells are immune cells whose main job is to make antibodies, proteins that protect the body by binding to foreign objects such as bacteria and viruses. B cells mature in the bone marrow, the tissue inside bones where all blood cells develop. B cells circulate in the bloodstream and also live in the spleen and in lymph nodes, small bean-shaped organs. A B cell has a large central nucleus surrounded by a thin layer of cytoplasm, the material between the cell membrane and the nucleus. A naive B cell, one that has not yet been activated, is coated with thousands of B cell receptors, which are antibodies anchored in its membrane. All the receptors on one B cell are the same, but a person's hundreds of millions of B cells can together bind a huge variety of molecules. A molecule that B cell receptors recognize is called an antigen. Only the B cells whose receptors fit a particular antigen are selected to multiply. These B cells are activated by binding to the antigen and then become plasma cells, which release a soluble form of the B cells' surface antibodies. One variety of B cell, the memory cell, forms after exposure to a pathogen, an agent that can cause disease, and responds rapidly to later exposures.
