The pancreatic A cell, or alpha cell, releases glucagon, a hormone that raises blood sugar levels. This cell lies at the edge of the pancreatic islets, or islets of Langerhans, which are clusters of cells in the pancreas that release hormones. Alpha cells make up about 20 percent of the cells in each islet. When blood glucose, the sugar the body uses for energy, drops between meals or during exercise, alpha cells release glucagon. Glucagon makes the liver break down glycogen, the stored form of glucose, and release more glucose into the blood. Glucagon also makes the liver turn amino acids from the blood into new glucose; amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Rising blood glucose then holds back further glucagon release; this is negative feedback, a control that keeps a response from going too far. Glucagon has the opposite effect of insulin, a pancreatic hormone that lowers blood glucose, and together the two keep blood glucose steady.
