Inner hair cells are the main sensor cells for hearing, and they form a single row of about 3,500 cells. They sit in the cochlea, the hearing portion of the inner ear. Each inner hair cell is bulb-shaped and lies in the organ of Corti, the site where sound is changed into nerve signals. Each cell's stereocilia, hair-like projections that help detect sound waves, are arranged from tallest to shortest and tied together by protein fibers called tethers. The stereocilia of inner hair cells reach into small dimples on the underside of the tectorial membrane, a structure lying above the hair cells. When pressure waves move the basilar membrane, the floor under the organ of Corti, the tectorial membrane slides across the stereocilia and bends them. When the stereocilia bend toward the tallest one, tension in the tethers opens ion channels, pores that let charged particles cross the cell membrane. This depolarizes the hair cell, making the voltage across its membrane less negative, and triggers nerve impulses in the nerve fibers attached to the cell. About 90 percent of the afferent neurons, which carry sound information from the cochlea to the brain, take their input from inner hair cells. Each inner hair cell responds to a particular frequency, which is heard as pitch, and the frequencies of neighboring cells differ by about 0.2 percent.
