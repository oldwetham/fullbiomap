Each adrenal gland helps control the stress response, blood pressure, blood volume and water balance by making hormones, substances that travel in the blood to act on cells in other parts of the body. Each gland is a wedge of tissue attached to the top of a kidney by a fibrous capsule. Inside, the gland has an outer cortex made of glandular tissue and an inner medulla made of nervous tissue. The cortex is made of layers: the outer zona glomerulosa makes hormones such as aldosterone; the next, the zona fasciculata, makes hormones such as cortisol; and the inner zona reticularis makes androgens, a class of sex hormones. Aldosterone makes the body hold on to sodium and fluid, raising blood volume and blood pressure. Cortisol raises the blood level of glucose, the simple sugar that cells use for energy. The cortex releases cortisol and related hormones during long-term stress, whereas the medulla responds to sudden, short-term stress. The medulla is made of large, irregularly shaped nerve cells called chromaffin cells, which make epinephrine, also called adrenaline, and norepinephrine. Epinephrine and norepinephrine speed up the heart and raise blood pressure, readying the body to fight a threat or run from it. The hypothalamus, a part of the brain, controls both parts of the gland in different ways: the cortex through adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), a pituitary hormone, and the medulla through nerve impulses.
