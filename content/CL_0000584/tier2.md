An enterocyte is a cell of the intestinal lining that absorbs nutrients from the lumen, the hollow interior of the intestine. In the small intestine, enterocytes make up most of the cell layer that covers the villi, projections of the inner lining. On the side facing the lumen, the enterocyte's cell membrane is folded into microvilli, small projections that give the cell ample surface for absorption. Seen together under a microscope, the microvilli look like a mass of bristles, called the brush border. Enterocytes in the small intestine also make digestive enzymes, proteins that speed up chemical reactions, and place the enzymes in the membranes of the microvilli. These enzymes finish digesting carbohydrates and proteins. Most nutrients are absorbed by transport mechanisms at the apical surface of enterocytes, the side that faces the lumen, but fats are an exception. Fats instead diffuse into the enterocyte, where they are rebuilt and packed with other lipids and proteins into chylomicrons, large compounds that transport lipids. The chylomicrons leave the cell by exocytosis, in which the cell exports material inside a membrane-bound sac, and pass into the lymphatic system.
