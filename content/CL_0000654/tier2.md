A primary oocyte is a cell that goes through meiosis, a special type of cell division, to produce an egg. Primary oocytes form from oogonia, the ovarian stem cells, in the ovary before birth. Each primary oocyte has 46 chromosomes, the structures in the nucleus that contain DNA. The cell copies its DNA and begins the first division of meiosis, but stops in prophase, the first stage of a division. Primary oocytes stay paused for years and resume meiosis only later, starting at puberty and continuing until a person is near menopause, the end of a female's reproductive functions. The number of primary oocytes in the ovaries keeps falling, to about 400,000 at puberty and to zero by the end of menopause. Each primary oocyte and its supporting cells form a follicle, and the oocyte remains a primary oocyte while the follicle develops through several stages. Each month from puberty on, follicle-stimulating hormone prompts several primary oocytes to start growing again. Just before ovulation, the release of an oocyte from the ovary, a surge of luteinizing hormone restarts meiosis in a primary oocyte, beginning its change into a secondary oocyte. The division is unequal: most of the cell's material goes to one cell, the secondary oocyte, while the other cell, a polar body, gets only one set of chromosomes and usually dies.
