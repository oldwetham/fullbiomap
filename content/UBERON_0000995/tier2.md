The uterus is the muscular organ in which a developing human, called an embryo and later a fetus, is nourished until birth. This hollow, thick-walled organ lies in the pelvic cavity, the space that houses the reproductive organs. Before a first pregnancy, the uterus is about the size and shape of a pear, with the narrow end pointing down. The uterus has three regions: the fundus at the top, the body in the middle, and the cervix, a narrow lower part that projects into the vagina. From outside to inside, the uterus has three layers: the perimetrium, the muscular myometrium and the endometrium. Most of the uterus is myometrium, whose muscle fibers run horizontally, vertically and diagonally, allowing strong contractions in labor and weaker ones that help expel menstrual blood. The endometrium has two layers: a basal layer, which lies against the myometrium and is not shed during menstruation, and a functional layer. Rising levels of estrogen and progesterone, sex hormones made by the ovaries, make the functional layer grow and thicken. A fertilized egg implants, or embeds itself, in the functional layer; if no egg is fertilized, only this layer is shed during menstruation. As pregnancy goes on, the uterus grows dramatically to hold the fetus.
