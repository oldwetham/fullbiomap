The spinal cord, a thick bundle of nerve tissue, carries information both ways: from the body to the brain, and from the brain back to the body. The spinal cord also controls reflexes, quick movements made without conscious thought, such as pulling a hand away from a hot object. Many reflexes are handled within the spinal cord itself, without involving the higher centers of the brain. Together, the brain and the spinal cord make up the central nervous system. The spinal cord lies within the vertebral column, the series of bones joined together as the backbone. The spinal cord runs from the base of the skull down to the level of the first lumbar vertebra, one of the bones of the lower back. The cord is divided into 31 segments, and each segment gives rise to a pair of spinal nerves, which run between the cord and the skin or muscles. In cross-section, the spinal cord looks like a white oval around a gray, butterfly-shaped core. The gray core, called gray matter, holds the cell bodies of nerve cells, while the white part, called white matter, is made of nerve fibers wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating substance. The back side of the cord mostly carries sensory information from the body to the brain, while the front side mainly carries signals that control movement from the brain to the body.
