Proximal tubule cells take back most of the dissolved substances filtered out by the kidney, a process called tubular reabsorption. These cells recover nearly all the filtered glucose, amino acids and vitamins, along with most of the filtered water, sodium and potassium. The proximal tubule receives this filtered fluid and does more reabsorption than any other part of the nephron, the kidney's urine-making unit. Proximal tubule cells form the tubule's wall, a simple cuboidal epithelium, meaning a single layer of cube-shaped cells. On the side facing the tubule's interior, each cell bears microvilli, thin projections that together form a brush border. The microvilli give the cells a large surface for absorbing and releasing substances such as sodium, chloride and glucose. Proximal tubule cells contain many mitochondria, the cell parts that produce the cell's energy (ATP), because the cells actively transport ions. Glucose enters each cell with sodium: both bind the same carrier proteins on the side facing the tubule's interior, and the inflowing sodium pulls glucose along. On the opposite side of the cell, sodium–potassium pumps, which use ATP to move sodium out and potassium in, supply the energy that moves glucose. While taking back sodium, proximal tubule cells also actively secrete hydrogen ions into the fluid that becomes urine, so the body gets rid of excess hydrogen ions.
