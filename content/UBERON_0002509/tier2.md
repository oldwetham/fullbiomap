Mesenteric lymph nodes filter lymph, a watery fluid that bathes tissues and organs, before the lymph returns to the blood. These nodes remove debris and pathogens, agents that can cause disease, from the lymph. Each node is also a site of immune responses carried out by T cells and B cells, two types of white blood cell. The mesenteric lymph nodes of the intestine are among the places where T cells detect antigens, foreign proteins that trigger an immune response. Mesenteric lymph nodes lie in the mesentery, a band of tissue that anchors most of the small intestine. Like other lymph nodes, each one is small and bean-shaped, usually less than 2.5 cm long. Each node is wrapped in a tough capsule of connective tissue, the tissue that holds organs in place, and extensions of the capsule divide the node into compartments. Inside the node, lymph seeps through connective tissue filled with white blood cells, which remove bacteria and viruses. Lymph then leaves through an efferent vessel at an indented region of the node called the hilum.
