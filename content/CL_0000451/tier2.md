Dendritic cells take up antigens, foreign molecules that immune cells react to. Dendritic cells then carry these antigens to lymph nodes, small bean-shaped organs, to stimulate an immune response. Dendritic cells stimulate this response by activating T cells, white blood cells that physically attack foreign or diseased cells. Dendritic cells also engulf pathogens, agents that can cause disease, along with cell debris. Dendritic cells live in the skin, where they are called Langerhans cells, and in the lining of the nose, lungs, stomach and intestines. In the spleen, dendritic cells help remove microbes and other materials, including dying red blood cells, from the blood. Dendritic cells develop from monocytes, cells found in the blood. In the large intestine, dendritic cells open tight junctions, the firm seals between neighboring cells of the gut lining, and extend probes into the gut's hollow interior to check microbial antigens. These dendritic cells, now carrying antigens, travel to nearby immune tissue in the gut lining, where T cells inspect the antigens.
