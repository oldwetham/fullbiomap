The seminal vesicle is a gland whose fluid becomes part of semen, the ejaculated mix of sperm and gland secretions. Together, the two seminal vesicles supply about 60 percent of the volume of semen. Each seminal vesicle lies behind the bladder and above the prostate gland. Each seminal vesicle has a short duct that joins the ductus deferens (vas deferens), the duct that carries sperm, to form an ejaculatory duct. As sperm pass through the ductus deferens during ejaculation, they mix with fluid from the seminal vesicle. Each ejaculatory duct then passes through the prostate and empties into the urethra, which carries both urine and semen in males. Seminal vesicle fluid is thick, yellowish and alkaline, meaning its pH is above 7. Because sperm can move only in an alkaline setting, this fluid helps reverse the acidity of the vagina. The fluid also contains large amounts of fructose, a simple sugar that sperm use to power their movement through the female reproductive tract. In addition, the fluid holds prostaglandins, hormones that act locally and help sperm move and survive.
