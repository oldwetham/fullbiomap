A neutrophil is a white blood cell that engulfs and digests pathogens, agents that can cause disease. Neutrophils respond rapidly to the site of an infection and are efficient phagocytes that prefer bacteria; a phagocyte is a cell that can surround and engulf a particle or another cell. Neutrophils are drawn from the bloodstream into infected tissues by chemotaxis, which means movement in response to chemicals. While macrophages, phagocytes found in several tissues, act like guards always on watch for infection, neutrophils are like reinforcements called into battle. Neutrophils are the most common white blood cells, normally 50–70 percent of the total, and each neutrophil is 10–12 µm across, much larger than a red blood cell. A neutrophil's nucleus has two to five lobes, more as the cell ages, and older neutrophils are often called polymorphonuclear, meaning having a nucleus with many forms. A neutrophil is named for its many fine granules, stores of chemicals inside the cell, because the granules show up most clearly with chemically neutral stains. A neutrophil's granules hold lysozyme, which breaks down bacterial cell walls; chemicals such as hydrogen peroxide; and defensins, proteins that puncture the membranes of bacteria and fungi so that the contents of those cells leak out. Neutrophils also contain lysosomes, the cell's digestive parts, which digest the pathogens a neutrophil engulfs. Like the other granular white blood cells, neutrophils are made in the red bone marrow, the tissue inside bones, and live only hours to days.
