Perineuronal satellite cells supply neurons, the nerve cells that carry electrical signals, with nutrients and structural support. Satellite cells are glial cells, the cells that provide a framework of tissue to support neurons. Satellite cells are one of the two kinds of glial cells in the peripheral nervous system (PNS): everything except the brain and spinal cord, which form the central nervous system (CNS). In the PNS, satellite cells do much the same support work that astrocytes, star-shaped cells, do in the CNS. Satellite cells surround the cell bodies of neurons, the parts that contain each neuron's nucleus. Satellite cells do this inside ganglia, which are clusters of neuron cell bodies in the PNS. In a dorsal root ganglion, the most common type of sensory ganglion, smaller satellite cells surround the large cell bodies of sensory neurons. The small, round nuclei of satellite cells can be seen ringing the neuron cell bodies, as if orbiting them. This appearance under the microscope is what gives satellite cells their name. Satellite cells develop from the neural crest, tissue in the embryo that migrates to form structures of the PNS.
