Cardiac pacemaker cells are specialized heart muscle cells that control the heartbeat and directly set the heart rate. Together they form the sinoatrial (SA) node, a clump of myocardial conducting cells, cells that carry electrical impulses through the heart and trigger its contraction. The SA node lies in the upper and rear walls of the right atrium, an upper chamber of the heart that receives blood. Each SA node cell is slender and spindle-shaped, and these cells sit in the center of the node, arranged around the node's artery. Pacemaker cells can fire electrical impulses on their own at set intervals, an ability called autorhythmicity. In SA node cells, this ability comes from spontaneous depolarization, in which sodium ions seep in through always-open channels and the voltage across the cell membrane rises. The SA node reaches threshold, the voltage at which an impulse begins, faster than any other group of conducting cells, and it starts the impulses that spread to the other conducting cells. Gap junctions, channels that carry current from one heart muscle cell to the next, link pacemaker cells to surrounding heart muscle cells, so each impulse spreads and the heart contracts in a coordinated way. Signals from the autonomic nervous system, the part of the nervous system that controls heart muscle, smooth muscle and glands, make pacemaker cells speed up or slow down the heart rate. Pacemaker cells also respond to hormones, substances the blood carries to cells in other parts of the body; some hormones adjust the heart rate to control blood pressure.
