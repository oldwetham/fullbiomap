A Sertoli cell is a supporting cell that nourishes germ cells, the developing sperm cells. Sertoli cells sit inside the seminiferous tubules, which make up most of the testis and produce sperm. Sertoli cells, also called sustentacular cells, are large and stretch from the outer edge of a seminiferous tubule to the lumen, the tubule's hollow center. Long and branching, Sertoli cells surround germ cells at every stage of development. Tight junctions, firm seals between neighboring cells, join Sertoli cells and form the blood–testis barrier, which keeps substances in the blood away from germ cells. The barrier also stops molecules on developing germ cells from escaping into the blood and prompting an autoimmune response, an immune response to the body's own cells. Sertoli cells release signaling molecules that promote sperm production and can control whether germ cells live or die. In the testes, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), a hormone that helps sex cells form and mature, binds mainly to Sertoli cells to boost sperm production. But when sperm numbers get too high, Sertoli cells release the hormone inhibin into the blood; inhibin holds back FSH release, so sperm production slows. In a male embryo, secretions from Sertoli cells trigger the breakdown of the Müllerian duct, the embryo's duct system that can form internal female reproductive structures.
