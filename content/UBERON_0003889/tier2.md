Each fallopian tube, also called a uterine tube or oviduct, carries the oocyte, or egg, from the ovary to the uterus. Each tube reaches from the uterus to the ovary but is not in contact with the ovary. The isthmus is the narrow end that joins the uterus, while the wide far end, the infundibulum, flares out into slender, finger-like projections called fimbriae. The middle region, called the ampulla, is where fertilization, the joining of a sperm and an egg, often takes place. The tube wall has three layers: an outer covering called the serosa, a middle layer of muscle, and an inner lining called the mucosa. Besides cells that secrete mucus, the mucosa has cells with cilia, short hair-like structures that can move substances along a cell's surface. At ovulation, when the ovary releases an oocyte, the fimbriae become more active and create currents that help draw the oocyte into the tube. Once the oocyte is inside the tube, muscle contractions and beating cilia move the oocyte slowly toward the uterus. If the oocyte is fertilized, the zygote, or fertilized egg, divides into two cells, then four, as it moves through the tube into the uterus.
