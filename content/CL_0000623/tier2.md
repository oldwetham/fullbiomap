Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes, a type of white blood cell with a large nucleus, that kill cells infected with viruses. NK cells take part in the innate immune response, the body's rapid but relatively nonspecific defense. An NK cell travels in the blood and carries cell-killing granules, small membrane-wrapped sacs that hold concentrated protein for release. NK cells are always active, but normal MHC I molecules (proteins on the surface of all cells with a nucleus) on a healthy cell switch off the NK cell's killing sequence. NK cells can, however, recognize and destroy virus-infected cells that lack MHC I molecules. An NK cell can induce apoptosis, a chain of events inside the target cell that causes the target cell's own death, in either of two ways. In the first, the NK cell displays fas ligand, a surface molecule that binds to the fas molecule on the infected cell and sends that cell signals to undergo apoptosis. In the second, the NK cell's granules release perforins, proteins that form pores in the infected cell's membrane, and granzymes, which pass through those pores and trigger apoptosis from inside. If an NK cell triggers apoptosis before a virus can make and assemble all its parts, no infectious virus is released from the infected cell, which prevents further infection. NK cells also help regulate immune responses by releasing cytokines, signaling molecules that let cells communicate over short distances, and by direct contact with other cells.
