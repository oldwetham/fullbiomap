The diaphragm is the main muscle of breathing and also separates the thoracic cavity, the chest space that holds the heart and lungs, from the abdomen. At rest the diaphragm is shaped like a dome, with a curved underside that forms the roof of the abdominal cavity, the space that holds the digestive organs. At the middle of the diaphragm is the central tendon, and the surrounding muscle attaches into this tendon. This muscle starts at the breastbone, the lower six ribs and the lumbar vertebrae of the lower back. The diaphragm has three openings for structures passing between the chest and the abdomen, and one of these openings lets through the inferior vena cava, the large vein that returns blood to the heart from the lower body. The esophagus passes through another opening, and the aorta, the largest artery in the body, passes through the last one. The phrenic nerve, the nerve that supplies the diaphragm, sets off the muscle contractions that drive breathing. During inspiration, or breathing in, the diaphragm contracts, flattens and drops toward the abdominal cavity; this movement helps enlarge the thoracic cavity. The bigger thoracic cavity lowers the air pressure inside the lungs, so air flows in.
