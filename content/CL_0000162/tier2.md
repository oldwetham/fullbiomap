A parietal cell secretes hydrochloric acid, the stomach's digestive acid, and makes intrinsic factor, which is essential for absorbing vitamin B12. Parietal cells lie mainly in the middle region of the gastric glands, which secrete a digestive fluid called gastric juice. Each parietal cell is pyramid-shaped and has abundant mitochondria, the cell parts that produce energy. A parietal cell also has a network of canaliculi, narrow channels where the cell's surface membrane folds inward. Parietal cells secrete hydrogen ions and chloride ions, charged atoms that combine inside the stomach to form hydrochloric acid. This acid gives the stomach contents their high acidity and is needed to activate pepsin, which digests protein. The acidity also kills much of the bacteria swallowed with food and helps denature proteins, making the proteins lose their shape and become easier to digest. Without intrinsic factor, the intestine cannot absorb vitamin B12, which the body needs for making mature red blood cells and for normal nerve function. Intrinsic factor binds to vitamin B12 and protects the vitamin from digestion. When food is present, the stomach releases gastrin, a hormone (chemical signal) that stimulates parietal cells to secrete acid.
