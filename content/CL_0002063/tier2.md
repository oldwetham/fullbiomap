Type II alveolar cells release pulmonary surfactant, which keeps the alveoli, the small, grape-like sacs of the lungs, from collapsing when you breathe out. Pulmonary surfactant is a mix of proteins and phospholipids, the main material of membranes. Type II alveolar cells are scattered among type I alveolar cells, which are squamous, or flat, cells of the alveoli. Each type II cell is cuboidal, or cube-shaped, and thicker than these squamous cells, with a rounded top that bulges above the surrounding lining. Short microvilli, projections that increase a cell's surface area, cover the type II cell's exposed surface. Type II alveolar cells adjust the fluid around the alveolar lining by releasing and recycling surfactant. They do this through special organelles, the small working parts inside a cell, called lamellar bodies. Water lining the alveoli creates surface tension, the pull at a liquid's surface that keeps the liquid's molecules from separating, and this tension resists the expansion of the alveoli. Surfactant works like a detergent to lower this surface tension, which makes the airways easier to inflate. Type II alveolar cells also help repair tissue, and after an injury they can turn into type I alveolar cells.
