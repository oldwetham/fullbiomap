An erythrocyte, or red blood cell, carries oxygen from the lungs to the body's tissues and brings some carbon dioxide waste back to the lungs. It forms in the bone marrow, the tissue inside bones, and then circulates through the body. As it matures in the red bone marrow, it pushes out its nucleus and most other organelles, the small working parts inside a cell. Lacking most organelles leaves it more room inside for hemoglobin, an oxygen-carrying compound. Each hemoglobin molecule holds four heme groups, iron-containing pigments that bind oxygen, so it can carry up to four oxygen molecules. Because it has no mitochondria, the organelles whose main job is making cellular energy, it uses none of the oxygen it carries and can deliver it all to the tissues. Its shape is a biconcave disk, which means it is plump around the edge and very thin in the center. This shape gives it more surface area for exchanging gases, relative to its volume, than a sphere of similar width would have. Structural proteins such as spectrin let it change shape to squeeze through capillaries, the smallest blood vessels. After up to 120 days in the blood, worn-out erythrocytes are removed by macrophages, large engulfing cells found mainly in the bone marrow, liver and spleen.
