The thymus is where T cells, white blood cells with an important role in the immune response, multiply and mature. Immature T cells, called thymocytes, leave the bone marrow, the tissue inside bones where blood cells develop, and mature mostly in the thymus. The thymus also makes hormones called thymosins, chemical signals that help T cells develop. The thymus has two lobes and sits in front of the heart, behind the sternum, or breastbone. The thymus is large and very active in infancy and early childhood, when the immune system is still developing, but shrinks in adulthood. A capsule of connective tissue, the tissue that holds organs in place, divides the thymus into lobules through extensions called trabeculae. The outer region of the thymus, called the cortex, holds large numbers of thymocytes. Before leaving the thymus, thymocytes move into the medulla, a region where cells are packed less densely. In the thymus, T cells are also selected so that they can attack pathogens, agents that can cause disease, without harming the body's own cells. This selection kills many thymocytes: only two percent of those that enter the thymus leave it as mature, working T cells.
