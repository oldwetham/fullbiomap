Ependymal cells filter blood to make cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), a clear liquid that surrounds the brain and spinal cord and acts as a shock absorber. These cells are a type of glia, the cells that support neurons; neurons are the cells that receive and send signals. Ependymal cells line each of the four ventricles, the open spaces inside the brain through which CSF flows. In the spinal cord, ependymal cells line the central canal, the hollow space within the cord. At the choroid plexus, a specialized structure in the ventricles, ependymal cells contact blood vessels and filter and absorb parts of the blood to make CSF. Ependymal cells form a single layer, tightly connected to neighboring cells. Each ependymal cell has cilia, short hair-like structures that extend from the cell and can move substances along its outer surface. The cilia sit on the cell's apical surface, the side that faces an open space, and help move CSF through the ventricles.
