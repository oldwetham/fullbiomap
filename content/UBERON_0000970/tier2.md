Each eye detects light, which gives us vision, the sense of sight. Each eye sits in a bony socket of the skull called the orbit, which surrounds and protects the eyeball and anchors the eye's soft tissues. Six extraocular muscles, which run from the bones of the orbit to the surface of the eyeball, move the eye within the orbit. The eye is a hollow ball made of three layers of tissue; the outer layer includes the white sclera and, at the front, the clear cornea, which helps focus light. The middle layer includes the choroid, which is rich in blood vessels and supplies the eyeball with blood. The innermost layer, lining the back of the eye, is the retina, whose light-sensing cells change light into nerve impulses. At the front of the eye is the iris, the colored muscle around the pupil, the central hole that lets light in; the iris narrows the pupil in bright light and widens it in dim light. The lens, a clear curved structure behind the cornea, focuses light on the retina, and muscles change the lens's shape so the lens can focus on near or far objects. Behind the lens, the space that reaches back to the retina is filled with a thick fluid called the vitreous humor. The optic nerve, the nerve responsible for vision, runs from the retina to the brain through an opening at the back of the orbit.
