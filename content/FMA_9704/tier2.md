Each ureter is a small tube that carries urine to the bladder from the renal pelvis, a large space in the center of the kidney where urine collects. From the renal pelvis, each ureter runs down the back wall of the abdomen. Each ureter enters the pelvis, the bony ring at the hips, and curves outward along the pelvic wall. Each ureter then turns inward and passes through the bladder wall at a slant, forming a one-way valve that keeps urine from flowing back. The wall of each ureter has three layers. The inner layer is transitional epithelium, a lining of stacked cells whose top cells change shape in response to urine. This inner layer makes mucus, a thick, slippery substance that coats and protects the surface of the lining's cells. The middle layer is smooth muscle, a kind of muscle that cannot be controlled consciously, with circular muscle on the inside and lengthwise muscle on the outside. These muscles produce peristalsis, wave-like movements of muscle tissue that push urine into the bladder without help from gravity. The outer layer is a supporting coat of fibrous connective tissue, a type of tissue that holds organs in place and connects them.
