A basophil is a white blood cell that releases chemicals to stimulate inflammation, a basic immune response. It is a granular leukocyte, a white blood cell with abundant granules inside it. Its granules release histamine, which contributes to inflammation, and heparin, which opposes blood clotting. Basophils hold serotonin as well as histamine, and they release these chemicals when properly stimulated. They also produce other inflammatory chemicals that recruit more white blood cells. Mast cells, immune cells that produce histamine, share the basophil's role of intensifying the inflammatory response, but the two cell types develop from different cell lines. Basophils are the least common white blood cells, typically less than one percent of the total, and measure 8–10 µm in diameter. Their large granules stain best with basic, or alkaline, stains and turn dark blue; they are so common that they can make the two-lobed nucleus hard to see. Basophils arise from myeloid stem cells, which also give rise to red blood cells and some other white blood cells.
