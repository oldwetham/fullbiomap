The small intestine is the body's main digestive organ, where most digestion and nearly all absorption, the uptake of nutrients by cells, take place. This hollow organ runs from the stomach to the large intestine. The small intestine is a coiled tube with three regions: the duodenum next to the stomach, then the jejunum, then the ileum. The lining of the small intestine is covered with villi (singular, villus), small hairlike projections that give the lining a furry texture. The absorptive cells of the lining have microvilli, tiny projections of the cell membrane. Enzymes, proteins that speed up chemical reactions, are fixed to the microvilli and finish digesting carbohydrates and proteins. Together with circular folds, which are deep folds in the lining, the villi and microvilli increase the absorbing surface more than 600-fold. Each villus contains a bed of tiny blood vessels and a lymph vessel called a lacteal. Sugars and amino acids from digested carbohydrates and proteins enter the blood directly, but fat breakdown products enter the lacteals. In segmentation, sections of the intestine contract and relax in turn, mixing food with digestive juices and pressing it against the lining for absorption.
