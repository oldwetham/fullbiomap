A peptic cell, or chief cell, makes pepsin, an enzyme that digests protein in the stomach. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up chemical reactions in the body. Peptic cells lie mostly near the base of the gastric glands, tube-shaped glands in the stomach lining. The secretions of peptic cells become part of gastric juice, the stomach's digestive fluid. Peptic cells release pepsin in an inactive form called pepsinogen. Making pepsin in this inactive form protects the peptic cells themselves, because pepsinogen does not work as an enzyme the way pepsin does. Hydrochloric acid, a digestive acid that parietal cells release, helps turn pepsinogen into pepsin. Pepsin also helps activate more pepsinogen, which in turn generates more pepsin. Protein digestion starts in the stomach, where pepsin and hydrochloric acid break proteins into smaller polypeptides, chains of amino acids, which then travel to the small intestine. Peptic cells also make gastric lipase, an enzyme that breaks down triglycerides, which are commonly called fats.
