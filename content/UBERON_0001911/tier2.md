The mammary gland makes milk to feed newborns. After childbirth, the gland makes and releases milk in response to an infant sucking at the nipple, a process called lactation. The mammary gland lies in the breast, over the pectoralis major, the large muscle on the chest. Mammary glands are modified sweat glands found in both sexes, but the glands usually work only in females. In the adult female breast, the glandular tissue forms 15 to 20 lobes that fan out around the nipple. Milk is made in alveoli, balloon-like structures lined with milk-secreting cells and wrapped in a net of cells that can contract. Tubes called lactiferous ducts carry milk from each lobe to the nipple, and just before the nipple each duct widens into a lactiferous sinus that stores milk. The female sex hormones estrogen and progesterone make the glandular tissue and ducts of the breast develop. The hormone prolactin helps the glandular tissue develop during pregnancy and stimulates milk production after the infant is born. When an infant suckles, the hormone oxytocin is released and makes the cells around the alveoli squeeze milk out toward the nipple.
