Corneocytes help protect the body from drying out and from pathogens, agents that can cause disease. Corneocytes are the dead cells of the stratum corneum, the outermost skin layer and the one exposed to the outside environment. The stratum corneum is usually 15 to 30 layers of cells thick. Each corneocyte is a flat cell with no nucleus, filled with keratin, a protein that makes skin hard and water-resistant. Corneocytes are the dead keratinocytes of this layer; a keratinocyte is a cell that makes and stores keratin. As keratinocytes die, their nuclei and other cell parts break down, leaving behind the keratin and cell membranes that form the stratum corneum. In the stratum corneum, layers of keratin and of glycolipids (combinations of carbohydrates and lipids) make the skin a barrier against water loss. The stratum corneum is too dry for bacteria to grow in, and shed corneocytes carry away bacteria and other pathogens. This dry, dead layer also shields the more delicate layers underneath from rubbing and scraping. Corneocytes are shed from time to time and replaced by cells pushed up from below, and the whole layer is replaced in about 4 weeks.
