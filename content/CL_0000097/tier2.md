A mast cell makes histamine and other molecules that promote inflammation, a basic immune response. In connective tissue, the tissue that holds in place and connects the body's organs, mast cells are among the most common cells. Mast cell precursors leave the bone marrow and mature in connective tissue and in mucosal tissue, the mucus-covered membranes that line tissues exposed to the outside. Once mature, mast cells are found in all tissues except the bloodstream. Inside, a mast cell has many granules, which are stores of chemical signals. These granules contain histamine and heparin, a substance that works against blood clotting. When irritated or damaged, mast cells release histamine, which causes vasodilation, the widening of blood vessels, and increases blood flow. Histamine also makes nearby capillaries, the smallest blood vessels, leakier, so fluid seeps out of these vessels. Basophils are white blood cells that release inflammatory chemicals, and mast cells were once thought to be basophils that had left the blood. However, the two cell types develop from different cell lines.
