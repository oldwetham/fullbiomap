Purkinje cells spread each electrical impulse to the contracting muscle cells of the ventricles, the large lower heart chambers that pump blood into arteries. Purkinje cells are the last link in the heart's conduction system, the specialized heart muscle cells that coordinate the contraction of the heart's chambers. At the heart's lower tip, Purkinje cells connect with the bundle branches, specialized conducting cells that run down through the wall between the ventricles. From the lower tip, Purkinje cells carry the impulse up through the ventricles' myocardium, the heart muscle cells that make up the middle layer and the bulk of the heart wall. Each Purkinje cell is a cylinder with a single nucleus, shorter than an ordinary heart muscle cell but nearly twice as wide. Purkinje cells are joined end to end in long rows, which run among ordinary heart muscle cells just beneath the endocardium, the innermost layer of the heart wall. Purkinje cells conduct impulses fast, and an impulse reaches all the muscle cells of the ventricles in about 75 milliseconds. Since the electrical stimulus to the ventricles begins at the lower tip, their contraction also starts there and moves toward the top of the heart. This works like squeezing a tube of toothpaste from the bottom, pushing blood out of the ventricles. Purkinje cells can also fire impulses on their own, but only 15–20 times per minute, more slowly than the bundle branches.
