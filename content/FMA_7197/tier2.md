The liver, the largest internal organ in humans, plays a key part in digesting fats by making bile, a digestive juice needed to break down the fat in food. As the body's chemical processing plant, the liver also packages nutrients from digestion, makes proteins carried in the blood, and gets rid of worn-out cell parts and wastes. Protected by the surrounding ribs, the liver lies in the upper right part of the abdomen, below the diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle under the lungs. Two main parts, or lobes, make up the liver: a large right lobe and a much smaller left lobe. Blood reaches the liver through the hepatic artery, which brings oxygen-rich blood from the heart, and through the hepatic portal vein, which brings nutrients absorbed from the small intestine. The liver controls how these nutrients are distributed to the rest of the body and removes toxic substances, such as drugs and alcohol. The liver's working units are lobules, where blood flows from the edge of each lobule to a central vein through open, porous blood spaces called sinusoids. Hepatocytes, the liver's main cells, are packed tightly around the sinusoids and process the nutrients, toxins and wastes carried by the blood. The sinusoids also contain star-shaped Kupffer cells, which remove dead red and white blood cells, bacteria and other foreign material that enter the sinusoids. Bile made by hepatocytes collects in bile canaliculi, small ducts between the hepatocytes, and then flows through larger ducts that join and leave the liver as the common hepatic duct.
