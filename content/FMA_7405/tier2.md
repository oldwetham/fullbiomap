Each main bronchus is a tube that carries air from the trachea, or windpipe, into a lung and back out again. The trachea splits into the right and left main bronchi at the carina, a raised structure whose nerve tissue triggers strong coughing if food or another foreign object is present. This split happens in the mediastinum, the space in the chest between the two lungs. The right main bronchus, which connects to the right lung, is wider but shorter than the left main bronchus. Each main bronchus enters its lung at the hilum, an indented region where blood vessels and nerves also enter the lung. Rings of cartilage, a firm but flexible tissue, support each main bronchus and stop the airway from collapsing, much like the rings of the trachea. The lining of each main bronchus contains goblet cells, which make mucus, a sticky fluid that traps tiny particles. Cilia, tiny hair-like projections on the lining, beat together to sweep the mucus and trapped particles up to the throat to be swallowed. Inside the lung, each main bronchus divides into secondary bronchi, then tertiary bronchi, and then bronchioles, small branches 1 mm or less across. The branches keep getting smaller until they end in tiny air sacs called alveoli.
