The pituitary gland, also called the hypophysis, makes, stores and releases hormones, substances that travel in the blood to act on cells in other parts of the body. Sometimes called the “master gland,” the pituitary sits at the base of the brain in the sella turcica, a groove of the sphenoid bone, which forms the central base of the skull. A stem called the infundibulum suspends the bean-sized gland from the hypothalamus, a hormone-making part of the brain. The gland has two lobes that grow from different kinds of embryonic tissue; the anterior lobe, toward the front, is glandular tissue that forms from the early digestive tract. The posterior lobe, toward the back, is instead made of nerve tissue. Releasing and inhibiting hormones from the hypothalamus travel to the anterior lobe through the hypophyseal portal system, a network of blood vessels, and make the lobe secrete more or less of its hormones. One of the anterior lobe's hormones, growth hormone, promotes tissue building and influences how the body uses nutrients. Some anterior lobe hormones are called tropic hormones because they turn other hormone-making glands on or off. Rather than making hormones, the posterior lobe stores ones made by the hypothalamus and releases them into the blood. One of these stored hormones, antidiuretic hormone (ADH), signals the kidneys to take back water.
