A pancreatic acinar cell releases digestive enzymes, proteins that speed up chemical reactions in the body. These enzymes help digest sugars, proteins and fats. Acinar cells make up most of the pancreas, an organ behind the stomach, and send their enzymes into tiny ducts that run between the cells. The cells are grouped into small grape-like clusters, each called an acinus, at the ends of the pancreatic ducts. As makers of the pancreas's digestive enzymes, acinar cells contain many ribosomes, the cell structures that build proteins. The inside of each cell is packed with neat rows of rough endoplasmic reticulum, whose outer surface is dotted with ribosomes. Each cell also contains dense secretory granules, small membrane-wrapped sacs filled with concentrated protein that will be released. The granules move toward the edge of the cell and, when stimulated, fuse with the cell's outer membrane to release their protein, a process called exocytosis. Enzymes that digest protein are released in inactive forms, because active ones would digest the pancreas itself. These enzymes become active in the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine.
