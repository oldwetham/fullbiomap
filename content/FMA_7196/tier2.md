The spleen filters pathogens, agents that can cause disease, out of the blood and removes worn-out or damaged blood cells. The spleen also stores red blood cells and platelets, small cell fragments that help prevent blood loss. The spleen lies in the upper left abdomen, behind the stomach and just below the diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle under the lungs. About 12 cm (5 in) long, the spleen is dark red because of its many blood vessels. A capsule of connective tissue, the tissue that holds organs in place, surrounds the spleen, which is made of two types of tissue: white pulp and red pulp. The white pulp is made mainly of lymphocytes, a type of white blood cell, gathered around arteries. The red pulp is made of cords of cells, such as lymphocytes, and venous sinuses, which are reservoirs filled with blood. Blood enters the spleen through the splenic artery, is filtered in the sinuses, and leaves through the splenic vein. The red pulp mainly filters the blood, while the white pulp is where T cells and B cells, the two basic types of lymphocyte, mount immune responses. Red blood cells last 120 days on average; then macrophages, large cells that engulf foreign particles, break down and recycle the old cells in the spleen and liver.
