A macrophage engulfs pathogens, which are agents that can cause disease, as well as debris, worn-out red blood cells and other dead or damaged cells. Macrophages are monocytes, a type of white blood cell, that have left the bloodstream. They live in many tissues of the body; some roam freely, while others stay fixed in place. Their names differ by tissue: Kupffer cells in the liver, alveolar macrophages in the lungs, and histiocytes in connective tissue, the tissue that holds the body's organs in place. A macrophage has an irregular shape and moves through tissues using pseudopodia, extensions of the cell. To engulf a pathogen, a macrophage uses phagocytosis: a section of its plasma membrane, the boundary between the cell and its surroundings, folds in around the pathogen. The folded-in section pinches off with the pathogen inside and fuses with a lysosome, a part of the cell that contains digestive enzymes; these enzymes then destroy the pathogen. When stimulated, macrophages release cytokines, small proteins that act as chemical messengers and recruit other immune cells to infected sites. They also stimulate T cells, white blood cells that physically attack foreign or diseased cells, to release cytokines that strengthen phagocytosis.
