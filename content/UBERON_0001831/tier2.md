Each parotid gland makes saliva, a digestive fluid, and sends it into the mouth. The parotid gland is an accessory organ: it helps with digestion but is not part of the digestive tract, the tube that runs from the mouth to the anus. The parotid glands are the biggest of the three pairs of salivary glands, and each sits in front of an ear. The parotid gland lies between the skin and the masseter, the main muscle for chewing. Saliva from the parotid gland enters the mouth through the parotid duct, which is near the second upper molar, a tooth for crushing and grinding food. The parotid gland makes a watery saliva that contains salivary amylase, which begins the digestion of carbohydrates in the mouth. Because this saliva is watery and has little mucus, a thick, slippery substance, the saliva mixes freely with food. The parotid gland receives nerve fibers from the otic ganglion, a cluster of nerve cell bodies. That ganglion in turn receives fibers from the glossopharyngeal nerve, a nerve that causes saliva production.
