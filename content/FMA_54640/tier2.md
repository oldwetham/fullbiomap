The tongue is important for tasting food and is also needed for chewing, swallowing and speech. The tongue sits over the floor of the mouth, and a dividing wall called the medial septum splits the tongue into symmetrical halves. The bulk of the tongue is skeletal muscle, the type of muscle under voluntary control. Each half of the tongue has the same set of extrinsic muscles, which start outside the tongue, and intrinsic muscles, which start within the tongue. The extrinsic muscles move the whole tongue in different directions, while the intrinsic muscles change its shape, for example by curling or flattening the tongue. The top and sides of the tongue are studded with papillae, small bump-like projections. Fungiform papillae contain taste buds, clusters of taste cells, while filiform papillae have touch receptors that help the tongue move food around. Taste buds in the front two-thirds of the tongue connect to the facial nerve, and those in the back third connect to the glossopharyngeal nerve. The hypoglossal nerve controls the tongue's muscles, except the palatoglossus, which is controlled by the vagus nerve.
