Osteoblasts are the bone cells responsible for forming new bone, a process called ossification. They are found in the growing parts of bone, including the periosteum, the fibrous membrane that covers a bone's outer surface. Osteoblasts are also found in the endosteum, the delicate lining of the cavity inside a bone. Osteoblasts make and release calcium salts and bone matrix, the nonliving material that fills the spaces between cells. The matrix hardens as mineral salts are laid down on its collagen fibers, strong and flexible protein fibers that resist stretching. As the matrix around an osteoblast hardens, the osteoblast becomes trapped inside and turns into an osteocyte, the main cell of mature bone. Because osteoblasts cannot divide, new ones develop from osteogenic cells, the only bone cells that divide. While osteoblasts keep forming new bone, osteoclasts keep breaking down old bone, and the balance between these two cell types constantly reshapes bone in small ways. When blood calcium is high, the hormone calcitonin makes osteoblasts more active, and they move calcium from the blood into the bone matrix. Osteoblasts also make osteocalcin, which prompts cells in the pancreas to make more insulin, a hormone that lowers blood glucose levels.
