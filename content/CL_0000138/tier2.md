A chondrocyte is a cartilage cell that makes the matrix and fibers of cartilage; the matrix is the material around the cells. Each chondrocyte sits in a small space in the matrix called a lacuna. Cartilage has no blood vessels, so all nutrients must spread through the matrix to reach the chondrocytes. In the developing skeleton, chondroblasts produce cartilage matrix, and once this matrix surrounds and isolates a chondroblast, the cell is called a chondrocyte. In the limb bones and some skull bones, chondrocytes first make a cartilage model of the future bone; bone tissue then slowly replaces this cartilage over many years. A chondrocyte keeps the ability to divide even while embedded in the cartilage matrix. Growth hormone makes chondrocytes multiply in the epiphyseal plates, or growth plates, which are sheets of cartilage in growing bones; as a result, long bones get longer. Bones stop growing in length, however, when the chondrocytes in the epiphyseal plate stop dividing and bone replaces the cartilage.
