The larynx produces the voice, keeps food and drink out of the trachea, or windpipe, and helps control how much air enters and leaves the lungs. Often called the voice box, the larynx is the air passage below the pharynx, or throat, and above the trachea. The larynx is built from nine pieces of cartilage, a tough but flexible tissue; muscles and ligaments link the pieces together. The largest piece is the thyroid cartilage, which includes the laryngeal prominence, or Adam's apple. The cricoid cartilage is a ring of cartilage in the larynx, wide at the back and thinner at the front. The true vocal cords are white folds of membrane whose outer edges are attached by muscle to cartilages of the larynx. To make sound, the vocal cords close together and vibrate as air from the lungs passes between the cords. The size of the vocal cords differs between people, giving voices different pitch ranges, and males tend to have larger folds and deeper voices. When you swallow, the larynx lifts and the epiglottis, a leaf-shaped piece of cartilage, swings down to close the opening to the trachea. The false vocal cords, another pair of membrane folds, play no part in making sound but help close off the larynx when food is swallowed.
