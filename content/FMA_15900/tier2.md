The urinary bladder is a stretchy, sac-like organ that stores urine and then releases the urine through the urethra, the tube leading to the outside of the body. Urine reaches the bladder through the two ureters, the tubes that carry urine from the kidneys. The bladder sits behind the pubic bone and in front of the rectum; in females, the bladder also lies in front of the uterus. The bladder's inner lining is transitional epithelium, a lining of stacked cells whose top cells change shape in response to urine. When the bladder is empty, its lining has many folds called rugae; the rugae and the transitional epithelium let the bladder expand as it fills. In adults, the volume of urine in the bladder ranges from nearly zero to 500–600 mL. Three openings in the bladder floor, two from the ureters and one into the urethra, mark the corners of a triangle called the trigone. The bladder wall contains the detrusor muscle, whose fibers run in all directions and contract to push urine out of the bladder. Urination starts with stretch sensors in the bladder wall, which send nerve signals to the spinal cord to trigger a reflex. In response, the detrusor muscle contracts while the internal urethral sphincter relaxes; this sphincter is the muscle where the bladder joins the urethra.
