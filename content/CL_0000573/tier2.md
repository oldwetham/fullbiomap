Cone cells detect bright light and are used for color vision during the day. They sit near the center of the retina, the layer of light-sensing and supporting cells that lines the back of the eye. The fovea, the spot at the center of the retina that is responsible for sharp vision, has a high density of cones. Cones and rods, named for how they look, are the retina's two types of photoreceptor, receptor cells specialized to respond to light. Cones are less sensitive to light than rods, but cones give sharper vision in both space and time. A cone's outer segment, the part where light is detected, is cone-shaped and holds light-sensitive pigments in inward folds of the cell membrane. There are three cone pigments, called opsins, each sensitive to a particular wavelength of light, and they are specialized to perceive red, green and blue. The brain compares the activity of the three kinds of cones to work out color. Light alters the pigment molecule, and this changes the cone's membrane potential, the voltage difference between the inside and outside of the cell. A cone passes its signals to bipolar cells, which relay them to ganglion cells, and the ganglion cells carry the signal to the brain.
