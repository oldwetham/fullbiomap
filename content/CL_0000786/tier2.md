A plasma cell is an immune cell that releases antibodies, proteins that protect the body by binding to foreign objects such as bacteria and viruses. Plasma cells often leave the secondary lymphoid organs, the sites of immune responses such as the lymph nodes and spleen, and move to the bone marrow. Plasma cells are also found in connective tissue, the tissue that holds the body's organs in place. A plasma cell develops from a B cell, an immune cell whose main job is making antibodies. Binding to an antigen, a foreign protein that triggers an immune response, turns the B cell into a plasma cell. Unlike an ordinary B cell, a plasma cell has a large amount of cytoplasm, the material between the cell membrane and the nucleus. This cytoplasm is packed with rough endoplasmic reticulum, the cell's protein-making machinery. The antibodies of a plasma cell have exactly the same antigen-binding site as those of the B cell that formed it. Released antibodies circulate freely and act independently of the plasma cells that made them. After releasing antibodies for a set period, a plasma cell dies, because most of the cell's energy goes into making antibodies, not into maintaining itself.
