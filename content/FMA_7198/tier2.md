The pancreas plays an important part in digestion and in controlling blood glucose; glucose is the preferred fuel for all body cells. The pancreas is a long, soft organ that lies crosswise behind the stomach and reaches from the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine, to the spleen. The pancreas has a head that nestles in the C-shaped curve of the duodenum, a body that extends to the left, and a tail that tapers to an end at the spleen. Most cells of the pancreas are exocrine, meaning they release their secretions through ducts: they send pancreatic juice through the pancreatic ducts into the small intestine. These exocrine cells sit in small grape-like clusters, each called an acinus (plural acini), at the ends of the pancreatic ducts. Pancreatic juice is rich in bicarbonate, which neutralizes chyme, the acidic mixture of partly digested food and stomach juices. Pancreatic enzymes, which are proteins that speed up chemical reactions, help digest sugars, proteins and fats. Scattered among the acini are small islands of endocrine cells, the islets of Langerhans, which release hormones into the blood, without ducts, to be carried to distant organs. Beta cells, the islet cells that make insulin, release it when blood glucose rises, as after a meal; insulin then lowers blood glucose by speeding glucose uptake into most body cells. In contrast, alpha cells, the islet cells that make glucagon, release it when blood glucose falls; glucagon then raises blood glucose by speeding the release of glucose from the liver.
