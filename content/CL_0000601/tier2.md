Outer hair cells fine-tune incoming sound waves, and about 12,000 of them are arranged in three or four rows. These cells sit in the cochlea, the hearing portion of the inner ear. Each outer hair cell lies in the organ of Corti, the site where sound is changed into nerve signals. Signals from the brain can change the length of outer hair cells, sharpening or dampening hair cell responses to certain frequencies, which are heard as pitch. This movement may actively help tune how sensitive the cochlea is and how well it tells frequencies apart. Each outer hair cell carries stereocilia, hair-like projections that help detect sound waves, and the stereocilia are arranged from tallest to shortest. The longer stereocilia of outer hair cells attach to the tectorial membrane, a structure that lies above the hair cells. Fluid waves made by sound flex the basilar membrane, the floor under the organ of Corti, and this bends the stereocilia attached to the tectorial membrane. The bending opens ion channels, pores that let charged particles cross the cell membrane, and the hair cell then sends a signal to the cochlear nerve. Outer hair cells connect to only 10 percent of the afferent neurons, which carry sound information from the cochlea to the brain.
