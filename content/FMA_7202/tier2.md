The gallbladder stores, concentrates, and releases bile, a digestive juice made by the liver. Bile helps digest fats through emulsification, the breaking of large fat drops into several smaller ones. The gallbladder is 8–10 cm long and sits in a shallow area on the back of the liver's right lobe. A layer of peritoneum, the membrane that covers the abdominal organs, forms the gallbladder's outer coat and holds the gallbladder against the liver. The gallbladder has three regions: the wide fundus tapers into the body, which narrows into the neck. The gallbladder's cystic duct joins the common hepatic duct from the liver to form the common bile duct, which carries bile into the small intestine. Between meals, bile is diverted into the gallbladder, where the bile is concentrated and stored until the next meal. The gallbladder's lining absorbs water from the bile, concentrating the bile up to 10-fold. When food reaches the small intestine, cells in the intestinal lining release a hormone called cholecystokinin, which makes the gallbladder contract. The gallbladder wall's middle layer is involuntary smooth muscle; when it contracts, bile is pushed through the cystic duct into the bile duct.
