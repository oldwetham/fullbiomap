A Schwann cell wraps around axons, the long extensions of nerve cells that conduct nerve impulses. Schwann cells do this in the peripheral nervous system, every part of the nervous system except the brain and spinal cord. A Schwann cell is a glial cell, one of the cells that provide a framework of tissue to support nerve cells. Schwann cells insulate axons with myelin, a fatty substance that lets electrical signals travel faster. Myelin works by keeping electrical current from leaking out of the axon, which speeds up the signal. Not every Schwann cell makes myelin, however: a non-myelinating Schwann cell instead wraps several thin axons. By contrast, a Schwann cell that makes myelin does so for only one axon, and the entire Schwann cell surrounds that axon. The myelin sheath is made of the Schwann cell's plasma membrane, the layer that separates a cell's contents from its surroundings, wrapped tightly around the axon. The Schwann cell's nucleus and cytoplasm, the material between the plasma membrane and the nucleus, form a bulge on one side of the myelin sheath. This bulge and the cell's outer membrane form the neurilemma, a tight covering around the myelin; the neurilemma plays an important role in the regrowth of nerve fibers.
