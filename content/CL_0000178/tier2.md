A Leydig cell makes testosterone, a hormone that helps sperm mature and the reproductive system grow. Leydig cells are also called interstitial cells because they lie between the seminiferous tubules, which make up most of the testis and produce sperm. Leydig cells make about 6 to 7 milligrams of testosterone a day. The male reproductive system needs this steady supply of testosterone to work properly. In a male embryo, Leydig cells release testosterone by the seventh week of development, and this early testosterone shapes the male sex organs. Testosterone levels stay low in childhood but rise during puberty, bringing body changes and starting spermatogenesis, the formation of new sperm. Testosterone in the blood also helps build muscle and bone and helps develop secondary sex characteristics, physical traits influenced by sex hormones. Leydig cells respond to luteinizing hormone (LH), which the pituitary gland at the base of the brain releases into the blood. On Leydig cells, LH binds to receptors, proteins that bind a hormone, and this triggers testosterone release. In turn, rising testosterone levels affect the release of LH, and this lowers Leydig cell activity and testosterone production.
