The prostate is a gland whose secretions make up about 30 percent of semen, the ejaculated mix of sperm and gland secretions. The prostate's thin, milky secretions help sperm move. The prostate sits at the base of the bladder, in front of the rectum. About the size of a walnut, the prostate wraps around the urethra, which carries both urine and semen in males. The prostate is made of both muscle and glandular tissue, and the muscle provides much of the force needed for ejaculation. The glandular tissue makes the prostate's fluid, which contains citrate, a nutrient, along with prostate-specific antigen (PSA). Tiny ducts carry the prostate's secretions into the urethra. After ejaculation, the prostate's fluid is key to making the semen first thicken and then turn liquid again. The brief thickening helps hold semen in the female reproductive tract, and once the semen is liquid again, sperm can travel farther in. PSA is an enzyme, a protein that speeds up chemical reactions; several minutes after ejaculation, PSA helps liquefy the semen.
