The large intestine, the last part of the digestive tract, finishes absorbing nutrients and water, makes certain vitamins, and forms and eliminates feces, the semisolid waste of digestion. The large intestine frames the small intestine on three sides. Although shorter than the small intestine, the large intestine is called large because it is more than twice as wide, about 3 inches across. The large intestine starts with the cecum, a sac-like pouch that takes in the contents of the ileum, the end of the small intestine, and keeps absorbing water and salts. The colon, the part between the cecum and the rectum, is divided into four regions: the ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid colon. The rectum leads into the anal canal, the last stretch of the digestive tract, which opens to the outside at the anus. In the colon wall, the lengthwise muscle forms just three bands, the teniae coli, and their contraction presses on the wall, creating a series of pouches called haustra. Chemical digestion in the large intestine is done entirely by bacteria inside the colon, which break down some of the leftover carbohydrates. Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus that eases the passage of feces, while absorptive cells called enterocytes take up water, salts and vitamins made by gut bacteria. Usually three or four times a day, during a meal or right after, strong waves called mass movements quickly push the contents from the middle of the transverse colon toward the rectum.
