Osteoclasts are the cells responsible for resorption, the breakdown of bone. Osteoclasts keep breaking down old bone while osteoblasts keep forming new bone, and the balance between these two cell types constantly reshapes bone in small ways. Osteoclasts are found on bone surfaces and come from monocytes and macrophages, two types of white blood cells. Each osteoclast is a large cell with up to 50 nuclei, the cell's control centers. Osteoclasts remove bone by releasing enzymes, which are proteins that speed up chemical reactions, along with acids. The enzymes and acids dissolve the bone's matrix, the nonliving material that fills the spaces between bone cells. Minerals freed from the matrix enter the blood, where they help control calcium levels in body fluids. When blood calcium is low, the parathyroid glands release parathyroid hormone (PTH), which makes osteoclasts multiply and work harder, so more calcium enters the blood. In contrast, calcitonin, a hormone from the thyroid gland, makes osteoclasts less active and lowers the calcium level in the blood.
