The heart is a muscular pump whose force keeps blood circulating to every tissue in the body. It sits in the mediastinum, the space between the lungs, where a tough membrane called the pericardium separates it from the other structures there. It is a four-chambered organ about the size and shape of a closed fist, with two-thirds of its mass to the left of the body's midline. Two of its chambers, the thin-walled atria, receive blood from the veins, and the other two, the thick-walled ventricles, pump blood forcefully out of the heart. Blood travels from the right atrium to the right ventricle, out to the lungs to pick up oxygen, back to the left atrium, and then to the left ventricle, which pumps it into the systemic circulation, the route to the rest of the body. Two kinds of valves keep blood flowing in the right direction: atrioventricular valves between the atria and ventricles, and semilunar valves at the bases of the large vessels that leave the ventricles. In each heartbeat, the myocardium, the thick layer of cardiac muscle cells in the heart wall, contracts in a phase called systole and then relaxes in a phase called diastole. The sinoatrial (SA) node, near the wall of the right atrium, is the heart's built-in pacemaker and sets the basic rhythm of the heartbeat; electrical pulses arise there on their own and make both atria contract together. Each pulse then reaches the atrioventricular (AV) node, between the right atrium and right ventricle, where it pauses so the atria can empty completely into the ventricles before the ventricles pump the blood out. The right and left coronary arteries supply the myocardium itself with blood.
